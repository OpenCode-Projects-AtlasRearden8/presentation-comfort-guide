Start Here: Presenting, Calm

You have been asked to give a talk. Or a report at work. Or a project update, a demo, a toast, a pitch, a training session for the new hires. And some part of you is already bracing.

This guide is for that part.

It is written for people who dread presenting — not because you are incapable, but because the standard advice you have been absorbing for years comes from people who enjoy being in front of a room, and it quietly assumes you do too. Their advice is something like "own the room" and "add some drama to your opening." Yours needs to be something like "structure a talk in one sitting so it stops hanging over you," and "write notes you can actually read while your heart is going," and "what do I do the moment a question lands that I cannot answer."

That is what is in here. Plain English, step by step, never condescending.

The promise

Everything in this guide is built around one idea: a talk you are comfortable giving is a talk that is structured, supported, and rehearsed just enough.

  • Structured — you can organize the whole talk in a single sitting, using the one-page outline worksheet at the back of this guide. The dread mostly comes from the talk living in your head as an unsorted pile. A structure makes it finite.
  • Supported — your slides carry a little of the load for you instead of becoming a second performance, and your speaker notes are readable at a glance under stress.
  • Rehearsed just enough — three rehearsals that prepare you, not twenty that grind you down. Plus one motion you can practice with an AI chat while you rehearse at your own desk, alone and unembarrassed.

One more promise: this guide never treats "I'm nervous" as a bug in you. Nerves are what a reasonable body does when it takes a thing seriously. We will not try to delete them. We will make sure they have nothing important to interrupt.

What is in the guide

  1. 01-one-sitting-outline.md — Structure a talk in one sitting. The one-hour method: five questions, three moves, one landing sentence. Includes the "tell a friend" test that catches most structural problems in five minutes.
  2. 02-slides-that-support.md — Slides that support instead of show off. A slide philosophy for quiet presenters: one idea per slide, readable from the back row, and when no slides at all is the right answer.
  3. 03-speaker-notes.md — Speaker notes you'll actually read. Chunky bullets, readable at arm's length under stress, cue words for the moments your hands will want something to do.
  4. 04-rehearse.md — Rehearsing without over-rehearsing. Three passes. Why the fourth feels productive and is actually erosion. What to do with the recorded footage of yourself that you will hate.
  5. 05-questions.md — Handling questions you can't answer. The honest-playbook: Naming what you don't know without apologizing your way off the stage, the three moves, the parking lot, and the follow-through.
  6. 06-freeze-ups.md — Recovering from freeze-ups kindly. Why the freeze happens, five recovery moves in the moment, a comeback script, and how to be kind to yourself after. Iceberg-style collapse is not permanent.
  7. 07-ai-sparring.md — AI as a rehearsal sparring partner. How to use a chat model (a cloud one, or a fully local one on your own machine) to critique your structure, play a tough audience, drill you on questions, and check your timing — with honest limits about what it cannot know about your room.
  8. worksheet.md — the one-page outline worksheet. Print it. Fill it in one sitting. That week's dread is done before your coffee gets cold.
  9. checklist.md — the day-of checklist. The night before, the morning of, and the first sixty seconds, so nothing important lives in your memory alone.
  10. glossary.md — plain-English definitions of the words people throw around.

How to use this guide

Three ways, pick the one that matches your week:

  • "I have a talk next week and I am spiral-adjacent." Read chapters 1, 4, and 5 over one calm sitting (this is maybe 40 minutes). Fill in the worksheet. Rehearse three passes using chapter 4. Take the day-of checklist with you.
  • "I want to change how I think about presenting first." Read the chapters in order, in one sitting if it helps you breathe. Then do the worksheet when you have a real talk to give.
  • "I need this specific fix right now." Every chapter stands alone. Skim the headings, jump to the part you need. The emergency page is chapter 6: if your freeze-up starts now, go there and work the moves.

A note about the printouts

The worksheet and the day-of checklist are meant to be printed. Every page in this site has a print-friendly layout — press Ctrl+P (or Cmd+P on a Mac) and the navigation gets out of the way. The worksheet works equally well in the docs/ source, in any text editor, on a napkin, or as bullet points on the whiteboard during your first sitting.
