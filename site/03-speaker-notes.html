Speaker Notes You Will Actually Read

Speaker notes fail quiet presenters in one reliable way: they are written as beautiful prose in the calm of the evening, and then they are read at 9:04 in the morning with dry hands, a distant projector hum, and adrenaline doing its thing. Prose does not survive that environment. Chunks do.

This chapter gives you a notes format that works under stress, a layout that works on a podium, and cue words for the four moments that most often go sideways.

The format: six-word bullets plus cue words

Every line of speaker notes has two families:

  • Content lines — 6–12 words, phrases only, never full sentences. Full sentences tempt you to read them aloud like a robot, and they break your flow the second you improvise.
  • Cue lines — instructions to yourself in [SQUARE BRACKETS].

Example page of notes for one slide:

SLIDE 4 — "The downtime: $40 per shift"

- last spring: line down 2 days
- 3 shifts × $40/shift × 2 days = ~$240
- story: Randy's walk to the warehouse (keep to 20 sec)
- bigger: 12 machines, ~$2,900/week all in
- [PAUSE after the number]
- transition: "so the fix isn't the paperwork — it's timing"

Rules of thumb:

  • One slide → maximum one page of notes. Two pages means the slide has too much on it or you are writing an essay.
  • The first line of every notes page is the slide title. It is your re-entry point if you freeze: you look down, see the title, and your next sentence is "so, this slide." More on that in chapter 6.
  • Include the small calculations. "3 × 40 × 2 = 240" beats "= about $240." Under stress you want arithmetic pre-digested, not done live.
  • Mark your stories with a time budget. "(keep to 20 sec)" — stories grow alarming legs when you are nervous. The budget helps you land them.
  • Last line of every page: the transition sentence you'll actually say. When a page ends with a written bridge, the changeover stops being a cliff edge.

The cue words

These four do most of the real work. Write them only where you want them to happen, not everywhere — a page full of cues becomes noise.

  • [PAUSE] — 2 seconds of silence after the thing they need to absorb. Under adrenaline you will want to fill silence; this line gives you permission not to.
  • [SLIDE] — the exact moment to advance. Prevents both the awkward "turn back" and the 4-slides-too-fast scramble.
  • [SLOW DOWN] — on your landing sentence and your one number. Nervous speakers accelerate exactly at the important parts; a visible instruction counteracts it.
  • [LOOK UP] — at the spots where you tend to read the slide instead of the room. usually right after your one big number or the first bullet.

Optional ones people add later: [QUESTION pause here], [DRINK a sip — you have water], [CHECK the time], [SKIP THIS if we're behind].

The podium layout

Test done in a real environment is worth more than a rule; here is the layout that quiet presenters report working:

  • Tablet or laptop with notes in large print, one page per screen, held at podium height — so a glance is a glance, not a bow.
  • Font size 18 point or more on the notes themselves. If your notes needs squinting, they are useless in the moment.
  • If paper is your thing: one page per slide, big font, numbered pages, and a rubber band around the stack. Numbering means that if a gust or a stumble sends it flying, you can rebuild your order in five seconds flat.
  • On the physical podium: notes to the left, water to the right, your one flash card with the landing sentence taped to the bottom edge of the notes.

Speaker view in the common apps

You do not need to memorize this list; you need to test it once in the actual room or with the actual equipment:

  • PowerPoint / Google Slides / Keynote: presenter view shows the current slide, the next slide, and your notes. Turn it on before people arrive. If presenter view puts notes on a screen the audience can see (it happens, more often than you'd guess), switch to the duplicate/extended-display setting and test it.
  • Zoom / Teams / Meet: share only the slide window, not the whole desktop — then your notes (and your tabs) are invisible. Test the share before the talk: open slides, share, look at what the "you are sharing" banner shows.
  • Projected-only setups with no second screen: print your notes. This is the one case where paper is the default, not the fallback.

Notes for the talks you know by heart (and the ones you don't)

Two honest categories:

  • A talk you have given five times: your notes can shrink to a single card with the three moves and the landing sentence. That is earned, not required. Quiet presenters often do BETTER with full notes forever, and that is a fine permanent choice. There is no planet where notes make you look weak; there is only a stage where you skip a move you never wrote down.
  • A talk you get dry hands about: full notes, exactly as above, plus the re-entry card built in chapter 6 (five lines on one flash card, which is the only place your freeze-up recovery plan is written).

The overnight rule

Write notes the same day you build the deck, while your reasoning is fresh, and then do not touch them the night before. Night-before note edits have a specific failure signature: everything sounds better reworded, the new wording has never been said out loud, and your mouth meets it for the first time in front of a room. Freeze-up chapter, point 1, is "the word you rehearsed is the word you say."

One-sentence summary

Notes are not a script; they are landmarks. Six-word bullets, cue words in brackets, one page per slide, sized to be read from arm's length — and the transition sentence written on the bottom of each page so no page ends in a cliff.
