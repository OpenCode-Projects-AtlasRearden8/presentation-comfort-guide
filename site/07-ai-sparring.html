AI as a Rehearsal Sparring Partner

One of the strangest, kindest gifts of this decade: you can now rehearse a talk against an audience that never gets bored, never rolls its eyes, never repeats anything it hears, and is available at 11 p.m. when the talk is at 9 a.m. and your stomach is doing gymnastics.

This chapter shows you how to use an AI chat — a cloud one, or a fully local one running on your own machine — as a sparring partner for the specific jobs a rehearsal partner does: poking holes in structure, playing a tough audience, drilling you on questions, and checking your timing. It also covers what AI can't do, so the tool stays a tool.

Why AI fits this job (when a human doesn't)

The problem with practicing on other humans is everything you already know:

  • Busy people give a real rehearsal the energy of "hurry up."
  • Kind people do not tell you the opening is foggy, and the fog survives to the room.
  • Scary people (your boss' boss) make a rehearsal worse than no rehearsal.
  • Asking for a second session takes social energy that presenting itself already taxed.

An AI chat has none of those problems. It will answer follow-ups instantly, follow-up, it gives specific critique if you ask for specifics, and — the crucial trait for a nervous presenter — it doesn't remember asking you to look at its slides over lunch later.

Two healthy limits to hold throughout: (1) an AI has never stood in your room and it doesn't know your audience, so treat its outputs as drafts to test, not verdicts; and (2) facts stay yours — an AI can suggest structure about your numbers but can't be the source of them, and anything factual in your talk should trace back to a document or a person you can name.

Setup: what to give it, in what order

The move that changes everything: don't just paste your slides and say "what do you think." You get vague replies to vague questions. Instead, work in this order — structure first, then words, then questions:

  1. Give it the frame: who's in the room, what should change afterward, how many minutes, how the talk will be delivered (live room / hybrid / video), and what you're dreading most.
  2. Give it the route: your landing sentence and your three move-sentences. Ask for structure critique before you show any actual slide content, so the critique is about the skeleton rather than about your typography.
  3. Only then paste notes or slide text, one move at a time.

You will get better results in 15 minutes of this order than in an hour of freeform "here is everything, is it good?"

Technique 1 — the structure critique (your "tell-a-friend," in writing)

Paste this prompt, filling the brackets (this and the other prompts below are copy-ready — paste them straight into any chat):

You're helping me prepare a short talk. Here is the context:

- Audience: [who's in the room, what they already believe]
- Desired change: [what they can do / decide differently after]
- Length: [X minutes], [live room / hybrid / chat + slides]
- I'm most worried about: [your honest note]

My planned structure:

- Landing sentence: [your one sentence]
- Move 1: [one sentence with a verb]
- Move 2: [one sentence]
- Move 3: [one sentence]

Please challenge this structure. In particular:
1. Where will this be confusing to [audience]?
2. Which move is doing work the audience won't understand yet at that point?
3. What is the single best change I could make to the route?
4. What question would you expect from a skeptic in this room?

Answer as a list of concrete, specific issues, in the order I should fix
them. Do not flatter me; a kind-but-blunt editorial note is welcome.

Then do something counter-intuitive: argue with it a little. Pick one piece of its critique and push: "I worry that drop would lose the setup for move 3 — what would you do instead?" The pushing is where the real thinking happens; the AI is warm-up weight resistance.

Technique 2 — the tough audience (it plays the room)

Once the route holds, have it play the room for practice:

Play my audience. You are three realistic people in the room I described:
[skeptic in the back / newer employee who's just hoping it's not more
paperwork / friendly senior person who asks the sharpest question].
Starting with the skeptic, give me questions about my talk as I answer
them. One question at a time. After each of my answers, rate it:
(a) does it actually answer the question, (b) does it hedge into mush
anywhere, (c) is there a shorter, more honest version? Then give the
next question.

This is question-time rehearsal for chapter 5's table of question types. Let it pitch the fear question ("does this mean my job?") too — practicing the fear question is something humans almost never offer you. When you hit a question you can't answer: practice the three moves out loud, and practice "I don't know, I'll find out" with a named follow-through date for the follow-through.

Technique 3 — the timing checker

Do a full-length spoken run at your desk against a timer, then compare notes:

Here's my rough timing in the run I just did (list it):

- opening: [2:00]
- move 1: [6:30]
- move 2: [7:10]
- move 3: [8:00]
- summary: [1:30]
- total: [25:10]  (slot is [20] minutes)

If I need to cut about [5] minutes, where's the most likely place that
cuts with the least damage? Ask me 2–3 follow-up questions about the
moves before answering, so you know what's load-bearing.

The phrase "what's load-bearing" is doing real work here. Any good presenter will cut what's not load-bearing; the model's useful trick is asking you the questions that identify it.

Technique 4 — the sparring partner for your speaker notes

Paste a single move's speaker notes and ask:

Here are speaker notes for one move of my talk (chunky bullets, in the
order I say them). Two jobs:
1. Say the move back to me in the natural voice an audience hears — not
   a script to read, but the likely shape of what I'd actually say.
2. Flag the bullets where someone like me would go off the rails under
   adrenaline: no number, missing slide advance, story without a time
   budget, missing [PAUSE].
Then give me a 3-item checklist to improve these notes.

The local, fully-private version

If the talk content is sensitive — an HR matter, unreleased financials, something private — you can run the whole sparring loop on a model that lives on your own machine and talks to nothing. The manual for that is the local-ai-handbook; the short version:

  • Install a local-model runner (Ollama or LM Studio are the common ones), download a small open model, and run it completely offline.
  • The same prompts above work; local models are a bit slower and wordier, so ask for shorter answers ("answer in 6 bullets max").
  • Zero data leaves your machine. For sensitive content, this is simply the right choice — there is no small-print judgment call to make.

The hard limits (read this even if you skip the rest)

  • It has never been in your room. Its "audience" is a bright abstraction. The real room has a hum, a clock, three people checking email, and one person who means well. Weigh its enthusiasm with a grain of salt: local chat models in particular are trained to be encouraging and will tell you "great structure!" if you ask weakly. Ask for problems, not praise.
  • It can be confidently wrong about facts. No number, date, name, or claim goes in your talk because a chat suggested it. Every fact traces to something you can name (a document, a report, a person) in the room.
  • It cannot measure your voice. Pacing, volume, where you trail off — these come from your recording (chapter 4, pass 2), not from a text model.
  • It is not a memory of your organization. It doesn't know that Randy is the one who talks during cost slides, or that the last presenter who tried a similar plan was politely murdered in Q&A. The humans you trust still hold that map. Use them for a late-stage sanity pass.

A good final rule: AI helps you think; a human feels the room. Use AI for rehearsal and structure; get one real human glance at the actual slides before you show them to anyone you need to impress.

One-sentence summary

An AI chat is the rehearsal partner who is always home: use it to stress-test your route, play your toughest audience, time-check your cuts, and pressure round your notes — while the facts stay yours and the room stays human.
