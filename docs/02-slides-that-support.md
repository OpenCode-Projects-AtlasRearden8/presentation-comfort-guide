# Slides That Support Instead of Show Off

Slides fail quiet presenters in two directions. Either they are empty (a wall of
paragraphs reading *at* the audience while you read *at* the screen), or they are
fancy (animations, clip-art fireworks, transitions that take longer than a sentence).
Both failures come from the same mistake: treating slides as the main event instead
of treating them as **furniture**.

The fixing idea is one sentence: **the slide's job is to help the audience follow
you, not to help you show off.** Everything in this chapter follows from that.

## The slide ladder

Every talk built from the one-sitting chapter needs a small, stable ladder of
slides. The count is set by the *moves*, not by your ambition:

- **1 title slide** — talk name, your name, the date, one line of "...for people who
  ...". Nothing else.
- **1 agenda slide** — your three moves, written as three short phrases. Not a
  paragraph. Not the landing sentence. This also gives the audience (and you) a
  promise-keeper: when you get lost later, everyone can see how far along you are.
- **One or two slides per move** — the move's sentence goes in the slide title;
  the evidence goes in the body. If a move needs more than two slides, that is a
  hint the move is really two moves, and that is okay — renumber.
- **1 summary slide** — the landing sentence, large and alone. Then your contact
  info and the words "Questions welcome."

For a 20-minute talk with three moves, the ladder is often **six or seven slides
total**. If you have thirty-two, that is not more information; that is less clarity.

## The five-word test

For every slide, do this: cover the body of the slide with your hand and read
**only the title**. Ask:

> Could a person in the audience who was mid-daydreaming see just this title and
> still roughly know where we are?

If the title is "Q3 Results" — no. They are lost until you speak. If the title is
"Q3: the machine downtime is costing us about $40 per shift" — yes. They are saved
even during the moment you are nervous and trailing off. **Slide titles do the
structural work.** They are a safety net for everyone, including you.

## One idea per slide

The single test for whether a slide is done: **if you feel no urge to say
"oh, and also—" while showing it, it is done.** One idea per slide; every other
idea is an example *inside* the slide's paragraph (a short one) or gets its own
slide.

The supporting test for body content:

- **Reads in 15 seconds or less.** If it takes 40, the audience is reading while
  you are talking, and neither of you wins.
- **Big enough from the back row.** Body text at 24 point or larger; titles at
  36 and up. If you must squeeze to fit everything, you have too much on it —
  split.
- **Contrast you can trust.** Dark text on a light background, or truly dark
  backgrounds with light text (watch the projector: many "dark backgrounds" come
  out grey in a bright room). Avoid colour-on-colour contrast that only works on
  your laptop screen.
- **No moving parts.** No builds, no animations, no "click to reveal." Every one of
  those is a thing that can mis-tap, out of order, and now the slide and your words
  disagree. People forgive a plain slide. They remember the one where the slide
  went sideways.

## What to do with data

Real numbers earn trust. A wall of raw numbers loses it. Three honest moves cover
most talks:

1. **One number, rests in the appendix.** Say: "If you want the whole table, it's
   on slide 14, and I can also just email it." Then show the one number that
   matters, big, with its source underneath in small text.
2. **Before / after.** Two rows, two columns, one highlighted cell. Works for
   almost everything.
3. **Trend with a label.** A simple line or bar with the takeaway *written on the
   slide*, not left for the audience to reverse-engineer: "Sales are flat in the
   north and down 20% since March in the south."

If a chart needs a paragraph to explain, append the paragraph to the slide's body
or split the chart. Do not stand at the front of the room narrating an axis label
into a microphone.

## Templates without trying

You do not need to design. Use one of these nearly-free routes:

- **Your organization's default template, if it exists and is clean.** Corporate
  templates exist so that you do not have to; using one also signals that your
  talk is in the family.
- **Build a tiny reusable template once.** One font choice (a common one like
  Arial, Calibri, or Georgia), two text sizes, one accent colour, one slide layout
  with title top-left. Save it as a template file. Now every future slide is a
  fill-in, not a design decision. Design decisions are exactly what you are
  trying to spend less of.
- **The no-design design.** White background, dark gray text, one accent colour
  used *only* on the landing slide and on numbers you want remembered.

Fonts to avoid: anything decorative, anything where the letters are all the same
width but different heights (you know the one), and 10-point anything.

## When no slides is the right answer

Sometimes the calmest option in the room is **no slides at all.** Good candidates:

- A ten-minute update with three decisions and next steps — a printed one-pager
  or a shared doc link is more useful than slides.
- A story-driven toast or memorial-style talk — no slide should be competing with
  what you are saying.
- A small room of fewer than about eight people where a document the group can
  look at together beats a wall-sized reproduction of the same.

If you have already said something like "I'm going to keep the slides minimal —
just a few signposts," nobody in the room will miss them. Observed fact: on many
quiet-presenter feedback cards, people write *the best part was that she just
talked to us like a person, with the deck supporting her*.

## Before-you-call-it-done checklist

Before you call the deck done, walk it with this list. Two minutes per full deck.

- [ ] Title slide has: talk name, your name, date, one-line purpose
- [ ] Agenda slide lists your moves as phrases, not sentences
- [ ] Every content slide title passes the five-word test
- [ ] Every slide has one idea, readable in 15 seconds
- [ ] No animations, no builds, no auto transitions
- [ ] Numbers each show a source (small text is fine; wrong numbers are not)
- [ ] Summary slide is only the landing sentence (plus "questions welcome")
- [ ] Print-out fallback exists: PDF copy on two computers and a USB stick
- [ ] Speaker-view test done with real audience-size text (chapter 3's setup)

## If you inherited someone else's bad deck

This happens to quiet presenters a lot: someone hands you a 42-slide monster and
says "mostly it's all there." Here is the fast, honest rescue:

1. Delete everything with no *singular* decision or event behind it. (You will
   usually delete 60%.)
2. From the survivors, write the move-sentences as new slide titles.
3. Anything that no longer has a home but you do not want to lose goes in the
   appendix after the summary slide. Appendices are allowed to be long; they are
   where things go so that they are not on stage.

You are not required to use the slide monster you were
handed. You are required to give a clear talk.

## The one-sentence summary

The slides are furniture: they hold the room steady while you are the one
speaking. Make them plain, make them few, make the titles carry the route — and
then go spend the rest of your energy on the part only you can do: being a person
in the room, saying true things on purpose.
