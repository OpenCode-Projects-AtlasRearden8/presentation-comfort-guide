# Glossary

Plain-English definitions of the words people toss around in presenting and
AI-adjacent conversations. No shame is attached to needing this page — jargon
is a tool some people use to sound experienced, and yours is the calm one who
actually explains things.

**Agenda slide** — One slide near the start listing your three moves as short
phrases. Promises the crowd what's coming, and gives you a visible map for the
whole talk.

**Adrenaline** — The body's "this matters" chemical. It narrows thought,
dries mouths, speeds speech, and makes your hands get creative. Not a fault; a
feature with a bad reputation. The techniques in this guide exist because of
adrenaline's side effects, not because you're broken.

**Appendix slides** — Slides after your summary slide containing backup
material, raw tables, the full method, anything you don't want to walk through
live but might need to show when a specific question shows up.

**Backup deck** — A PDF export of your slides that will open on any machine,
including the borrowed hotel laptop that cannot run PowerPoint.

**Before/after slide** — Two columns; "the old way" and "the new way." The
most reliable data slide a room can read in 10 seconds.

**Build (animation)** — The thing where a slide reveals bullets one at a time
as you click. Looks fancy, misfires at the worst time, and turns a calm talk
into a TV remote mishap. This guide says no builds.

**Clone deck / duplicate file** — A copy of your slide file saved in a second
place. Cheap insurance for the day a laptop dies in a hallway.

**Cue words** — Short bracketed instructions to yourself in speaker notes:
[PAUSE], [SLIDE], [SLOW DOWN], [LOOK UP], [SKIP THIS if we're behind].

**Flash card** — A single card, physical or digital, with the exact sentences
you most need under adrenaline: the re-entry moves from chapter 6, the
landing sentence, and (optionally) your three questions-time moves.

**Front-door pass** — The rehearsal from chapter 4 where you only practice
the first 90 seconds and then actually walk into a room to give your opening
as if people are there. The highest-value rehearsal for a nervous presenter.

**Hybrid meeting** — Some people in a room, some on a video call. The hardest
format for question time (two rooms with different attention rules); plan the
order of who you look at and who you listen to.

**Landing sentence** — The one sentence you want everyone to remember on the
drive home. It lives on your summary slide, in your closing notes, and in
your flash card rests at the bottom of your speaker notes pages.

**The ladder** — The talk's slide structure from chapter 2: title, agenda,
1–2 slides per move, summary. Real count often 6–8 slides for a 20-minute
talk. Small on purpose.

**Local model / local AI** — An AI model running entirely on your own
computer via a tool like Ollama or LM Studio. No data leaves the machine;
this is the right choice for private talk content. See the
**local-ai-handbook**.

**Open questions** — An honest list of things nobody in the room has answered
yet. Borrowed from good meeting notes practice: listing open questions shows
you're thinking, not bluffing. Ties to the questions chapter, and to the
meeting-notes-summarizer-cli report format.

**Parking lot** — A visible place (usually the summary slide margin) where
you write the names of real questions that deserve more than the 30 seconds
left in the session. You go back at the end, on purpose, every time.

**Presenter view** — The mode in PowerPoint / Keynote / Google Slides where
the room sees your slides but you see the current slide, the next slide, and
your notes, all on your own screen. Turn it on before anyone arrives.

**Prompt** — The text you give an AI chat in one go. The prompts in this
guide are pre-written for you: structure critique, tough-audience role-play,
timing check, speaker notes pressure-round.

**Re-entry point** — The slide title (in your notes as the first line of
every page). When a freeze-up hits, reading it aloud is a working way to get
back into the talk, because reading survives adrenaline better than
composition does.

**Rehearsal pass** — One run-through with a specific job (structure pass,
full pass, front-door pass). Three passes; a fourth pass is optional joy.
Chapter 4 explains the over-rehearsal flags that tell you to stop.

**Route** — The order and shape of your three moves: staircase, trailhead, or
problem-board (chapter 1). What you memorize, instead of memorizing a script.

**The "road," not route** — What over-rehearsal gives you: a memorized sequence of
exact sentences. Fragile under adrenaline, because one lost phrase has no
replacement. Memorize the route, not the road.

**Scratchpad (working memory)** — The brain's short-term assembly line where
you build sentences as you speak. Under adrenaline it throttles: that's why
composition fails and reading survives, and why the recovery moves from
chapter 6 are reading-based.

**Speaker notes** — One page per slide, big text, six-word content bullets,
cue words in brackets, the transition sentence at the bottom of each page.
Built in chapter 3.

**Sparring partner** — The AI chat you rehearse against (chapter 7): structure
critique, role-played tough audience, timing check, notes pressure-round.

**Staircase** — The route where the moves go least-to-most surprising. The
easiest and calmest of the three routes; the quiet presenter's default.

**Summary slide** — Your last content slide, showing only the landing
sentence (plus "questions welcome"). Also where the parking lot lives when
you promise it visibly.

**Tell-a-friend test** — The five-minute structural check from chapter 1: say
your structure to one real person (or one wall or one cat) and see what
confuses them. Catches route problems that slide-polishing never will.

**Trailhead** — The route that opens with a story, then uses the moves as
evidence. Memorable, mid-difficulty; do it only if you really know the story
cold.

**Three moves** — The three sentences with verbs that carry your talk's body.
One per section. The whole reason this guide works for people who dread
presenting: three is short enough to remember under adrenaline.

**White room** — What your mind looks like from the inside during a freeze:
nothing there yet. From the seats, it is just a person pausing. The gap has
two completely different sizes depending on which side you're viewing from.
