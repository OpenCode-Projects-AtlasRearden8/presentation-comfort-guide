# Handling Questions You Can't Answer

Here is a secret that experienced presenters know and nervous ones usually don't:
**"I don't know" is a completely respectable sentence in a professional room.**
What actually damages trust is pretending: the made-up number said with confidence,
the bluff about a system you have never used, the long foggy answer built to hide
a shrug. People remember bluffs. They also remember the honest, specific person
who said "I don't know — here's who does."

This chapter is a playbook for the moments that scare quiet presenters most: the
question you cannot answer, the question that is really an objection, and the
question you do not fully understand. None of them require genius. All of them
require a script you already know.

## The three moves

When a question lands and you do not know the answer, there are exactly three
things to do, in this order:

1. **Release.** Say the honest thing, out loud, without apologizing three times:
   "I don't have that number with me."
2. **Bridge.** Say what you *do* know, or what you can offer, in one sentence:
   "What I can tell you is the trend we saw in Q2 — want me to sketch that?"
3. **Handoff or follow-up.** Either point at the right resource, or commit to a
   follow-through: "Marta has the current version of that — she's in the room
   now. Otherwise, I'll find out and send it to you by Friday."

That is the whole technique. The silence after step 3 belongs to the audience;
you have done your part. The trap to avoid is filler: the nervous "great question!
so, um, that's a really interesting one, there are a lot of angles..." which
signals *I'm buying time and you know it*. Two honest sentences are stronger
than six foggy ones.

Write steps 1–3 on a real card and keep it with your notes. Chapter 6's flash
card and your questions card can live in the same rubber-banded stack.

## What kind of question is it?

Most questions in any room are one of five kinds. Sort quickly, then use the
matching move. (This is also your AI-rehearsal script in chapter 7 — a chat
model is very good at throwing all five kinds at you.)

| Question type | What it actually is | Your move |
|---|---|---|
| Information | "What's the actual cost?" | Answer if you have it; otherwise the three moves |
| Extension | "Will this also work for the South site?" | Answer with your known scope first, then the three moves if outside it |
| Objection | "Won't this just add paperwork?" | Name the objection honestly, then answer the concern, not the phrasing |
| Fear | "Does this mean my job?" | Answer the fear directly and plainly — never a euphemism |
| Out of scope / defense | "So why did this reorg happen in March?" | Name the boundary respectfully, offer a route to the right owner |

For the objection ("won't this just add paperwork"), the dangerous instinct is to
defend your whole plan sentence-by-sentence at length. The calm move is to
**agree with the part that's true, then answer the concern**: "You're right —
it does add one form. That's the paper cost. The back side is that we stop
re-entering the same number in three systems, which is where the real time went.
Both are true; here's the trade-off we chose."

For the fear question ("does this mean my job?"), the only acceptable answer is
**plain and directly addressed**, even if the plain answer is "I don't have a
final answer to that, and I'm not going to soften it into mush — what I know is
X, what's still open is Y, and the decision on Y is Z's by [date]." Vagueness
from a presenter on a fear question loses the room.

For the out-of-scope question, a boundary named kindly is better than a
circuitous answer: "That one's really more of a reorg question than a timing
question — I can say what I know about the piece I was in, but you'll get a
better answer from [person]."

## The parking lot

For questions that deserve real attention but not mid-talk attention, use the
parking lot out loud — it is a respectable move in any room, and it is calmer
than chasing every tangent:

> "That's a real one and it deserves more than 30 seconds. I'm going to park it
> — you'll see it on the summary slide — and if it's still standing at the end,
> I'll take it first."

Then actually write it on the summary slide (or the agenda slide's margin) while
you say that. The parking lot works because it has a *visible* place to live.
Two rules:

- **Park a maximum of three items.** The fourth goes to: "I'm going to follow up
  with you directly on that one after we wrap."
- **Go back to the lot at the end, every time.** If you never go back, everyone
  learns your parking lot is decorative and stops trusting every decent move
  you make.

## The question you don't fully understand

Say so, without drama: "I want to make sure I'm answering the thing you're
actually asking — are you asking about the timing, or about the budget line?"

This is not stalling. Rewording someone's question into your own words is one of
the best moves in any professional conversation, and it buys you what you need
(three seconds of thinking). One rephrase, not three.

## Question time structure

- **Take questions in the space you said you'd take them**, not in a panic at
  the end. "I'll take two questions at the 10-minute mark and the rest at the
  end" shows structure instead of luck.
- **Repeat or rephrase the question into your microphone** if the room is larger
  than a huddle. In a small room, skip it; repeating every question out loud in
  a huddle feels like you are narrating them.
- **After you answer, stop.** Do not fill the silence after your answer with a
  second, worse answer. Say "yes?" with your eyes and wait.
- **Close question time on purpose, not on exhaustion:** "We're at time; I'll
  stay around for another few minutes if something didn't get said" — then be
  genuinely available for another few minutes. This footnote catches the best
  questions, because they so often come from people who did not want to raise
  their hand in the room.

## The follow-through system

The follow-up promises you make in Q&A are a tiny, high-trust to-do list — and
they are exactly the *same* kind of artifact as the "open questions" section in
your meeting notes. This is the part where the guide connects to the
**meeting-notes-summarizer-cli**:

- Right after the talk (or that evening, while memory is fresh), type a few raw
  lines into a notes file: the questions you parked, the follow-ups you promised,
  who asked, and by-when.
- Run the summarizer on that file. It produces a clean **decisions / open
  questions / action items** report — and the action-items section is your
  to-do list for the next 48 hours. (It even converts by-Friday dates into
  reminders.)
- Executing two follow-ups *on time* builds you a reputation as someone whose
  "I don't know, I'll find out" is real. It also renovates fear into procedure:
  from now on, the scary part of Q&A is not the question; it is just a task that
  will show up neatly in a list.

## If the question is rude, or trying to win

Some rooms have a person who wants to win and not to understand. You will not
out-argue them in a room that did not come to watch you argue. Calm moves:

- **Name what you can name, once:** "I think we see the trade-off differently."
- **Do not match heat with heat.** Take your time; you owe calm, not speed: "I'm
  going to answer that one once, and then I want to leave room for other
  people's questions."
- **Do not take the bait of a personal jab**; keep the answer about the *work*.
  "I can't speak to motives; what I can speak to is the data, which says..."
- If a room goes truly hostile (rare, but real), the exit is structure: "We've
  got five minutes left and I want to make room for two more questions; I'm
  happy to keep working through this one with you after we wrap."

## One-sentence summary

"I don't know, I'll find out" — said honestly, followed up on record, written
into a real to-do list — is a stronger professional sentence than almost any
answer you could have bluffed.
