# Structure a Talk in One Sitting

A talk becomes scary when it stays a fog: "somewhere in there is a thing I have to say
and I have to remember all of it and it has to be good." Fog does not shrink by
thinking harder. It shrinks when you make decisions. This chapter is a one-sitting
method for making every decision a talk actually needs — in about ninety minutes —
so the fog becomes a list.

You will need: one hour and a half of relatively quiet, something to write on, and
permission to produce a rough-shaped draft that is *allowed to be ordinary*. Ordinary
is fine. Ordinary is what gets delivered.

## Before the sitting: know what the talk is *for*

A talk has one job: **move a group of specific people from not-knowing-something to
acting-differently.** Not "inform." Not "share." A real difference you can point at.
That difference is what we will write on the worksheet, in one sentence, before
anything else gets decided.

If nobody has told you what the talk is for, ask. A single message to whoever invited
you: "What do you want people to be able to do after my talk that they can't do
now?" Their answer does more for your structure than any book of tips.

## Step 1 — The five questions (15 minutes)

Write answers to these five questions. One or two lines each. Consistency of spelling does not matter; slight messiness actually helps.

1. **Who is in the room, and what do they already believe?** Not their job titles —
   their *understanding*. Do these people already agree with the change? Are six of
   them going to think this talk is about them? Is there someone in the back who has
   done this longer than you?
2. **What should be different about them afterward?** One specific change. If you
   find yourself writing three, pick one; the other two are now examples inside the
3. **What do they need to hear in order to believe it?** This is your content. Not
   everything you know — only the pieces that make the change credible. Everything
   else in your head stays in your head with our blessing.
4. **What can go wrong?** The objection, the misunderstanding, the thing your boss'
   boss will ask, the fear ("does this change mean my job?"). Write down the two or
   three worst. These are not decorations; they are ammunition, and chapter 5 is
   mostly about using it at question time.
5. **What is the one sentence you want them to remember on the drive home?** This
   will become your *landing sentence*, and we will write it in step 4.

## Step 2 — The three moves (30 minutes)

Almost every good talk has three moving parts between its opening and its landing.
This is not a superstition; it is a convenience. Three is short enough to remember
and long enough to develop one idea. (Four is also fine. Five starts to blur.)

Your talk, roughly:

- **A warm open** (2–3 minutes): one true sentence that establishes why the topic
   matters *to these people*, and what you will cover. Do not open with a joke you are
   not sure of. Open with something honest that is already true.
- **Move 1, Move 2, Move 3** (each 5–8 minutes): each move is one idea that brings
   the audience one step closer to acting different. Write each move as one
   sentence, then under it two or three *evidence pieces* — a story, a number, a
   before-and-after, a real example, a demo beat.
- **A kind close** (2–4 minutes): the landing sentence said plainly, plus one
   direction for what they should do next, plus the genuine sentence: *thank you for
   your attention; I take questions seriously.* Then you stop talking.

Write your three moves as three sentences. Use a verb. "Move 1: show why the current
process quietly costs more than we think." That verb fixes the slide that follows
it, the notes that follow the slide, and the way you will rehearse.

## Step 3 — The route (15 minutes)

Decide how the moves are arranged. Three honest options, in order of difficulty:

- **The staircase**: move 1 is the least surprising, move 3 is the most surprising.
  Easiest for you; audience can already predict it; very low risk, low spice.
  Choose it when you are not sure, or when the room is already wary of change.
- **The trailhead**: open with the story, then use moves as evidence, then land.
  Middle difficulty, higher memorability, depends on you having one story you
  genuinely know cold.
- **The problem-board**: put the biggest objection in the *middle* where people are
  most alert, and lead the room through it. Highest difficulty; do not choose it
  while nervous; do not choose it when the audience does not already lean your way.

There is no shame in the staircase. The staircase given calmly with real numbers
beats a dramatic problem-board given at 92% effort and a shaking paper.

## Step 4 — The landing sentence (10 minutes)

Write **one sentence** that says what you want them to remember on the drive home.
Read it out loud. Then do the three-bullet check:

- Does it mention the audience, not only the topic? (Not "our inventory system" but
   "we stop entering the same number in two systems.")
- Could you say it in 8 seconds? A sentence that takes 20 seconds to say is three
- Does it include a direction (do this, watch this, decide this, ask this)?

This is the sentence that goes at the **bottom of your slides**, at the end of your

## Step 5 — The tell-a-friend test (10 minutes)

Now do the fastest structural audit that exists. Say this to a friend, a spouse, a
coworker — or out loud to a wall; your cat is also a valid participant:

1. **What is the talk about?** They should be able to summarize your landing
2. **Does it make sense?** If somebody in your audience is going to hear it, they
3. **What is the best reason *not* to believe it?** If they cannot think of any,
   return to question 4 above, and add what they say into your "what can go
4. **How long would that take you to actually do?** If the answer makes you
   flinch, trim a move.

A five-minute conversation with one person will catch structural problems that
twenty passes over your slides would not. Doing this step on paper alone is fine
and still better than nothing.

## After the sitting

- **Do not expand the deck tonight.** Tonight, the slide count should be based on
  the moves, not your ambition. If you have 3 moves, you can budget 6–9 content
  slides, plus a title slide, an agenda slide covering what you'll cover, and a
  summary slide with the landing sentence. Chapter 2 tells you how to make those.
- **Start the speaker notes now** (chapter 3) while the material is still warm in
  your head. Notes written during the sitting are twice as good as notes written
  the week of.
- **Calendar the rehearsals** from chapter 4 next to the talk day. Three rehearsals,
  spaced.

## The 90-minute recap

| Time | Do |
|---|---|
| 0–15 min | The five questions |
| 15–45 min | Three moves, each with evidence |
| 45–60 min | Route choice + landing sentence |
| 60–70 min | tell-a-friend check (or wall version) |
| 70–90 min | start speaker notes (chapter 3) |

One sitting, then it is finished until rehearsal. The talk is no longer hanging
over you. It is a list with three arrows at the top.
