# Rehearsing Without Over-Rehearsing

There is a specific quiet-presenter doom loop: the talk goes badly in your head at
2 a.m., so you rehearse again. The extra rehearsal makes it feel *less* solid, so
you rehearse again. By rehearsal number twelve you have memorized sentence shapes
rather than ideas, and if anyone interrupts you in real life mid-paragraph, your
talk collapses inside you.

The way out is not "don't rehearse." Rehearsal is genuinely useful. The way out is
**rehearsing the right three times, spaced out, with a specific job assigned to
each one** — and treating any rehearsal beyond that as optional joy, not duty.

## The three-pass method

| Pass | When | Job | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Structure pass | 4–6 days before | Does the *route* make sense? | 15–20 min |
| 2. Full pass | 2–3 days before | Slides, notes, timing | full length |
| 3. Front-door pass | 1 day before (not the morning of) | Hand-over-the-talker moves | 10–15 min |

### Pass 1 — the structure pass

Stand up (this matters: your body remembers a talk better on its feet), hold your
notes page for the three moves only, and **talk through the whole talk with no
slides**. Your only job is to notice where "hmm, that feels foggy." You are not
fixing word choice. You are checking whether the route holds: does the open make
sense, does each move actually lead to the next, does the ending *land*.

When a section feels foggy, the fix is almost always the same: say the move-sentence
out loud before the fog section: "So the second question is: why now?" Saying the
signpost out loud clears fog for the whole room.

### Pass 2 — the full pass

Full dress: slides advancing, notes in real position, time tracked with a real
timer. Two extra jobs this pass:

1. **Check timing against the real schedule.** If your slot is 20 minutes and the
   pass lands at 24, decide *now* what you cut that you'd cut in the room — and
   mark that cut in your notes as [SKIP THIS if we're behind]. Do not decide the
   cut in the room.
2. **Record it.** One take, audio alone is enough (a voice memo is easier to face
   than video). Then do the following review, and only this review, once:
   - Watch/listen for the **first 3 minutes only**, checking: did you say the
     landing sentence topic up front? Do you *say what the talk is for* before
     minute 2?
   - Listen for the two places you went faster than the words needed. Mark those
     spots in your notes with [SLOW DOWN].
   - Stop reviewing. One listen is informative; three listens are corrosive.

#### The recording kindness

You will hate your recorded voice. This is universal, true for professional
presenters, and it means nothing about how you actually come across in the room.
Set the
recording aside after the two checks above. If you cannot bear to listen at all,
record only the opening 3 minutes.

### Pass 3 — the front-door pass

This pass has one job: **the first 90 seconds.** Because here is the physics of a
talk: the most dangerous nerves arrive at the start while you are still finding
your feet, so spending a rehearsal ONLY on the start means you will never trot
through the mushy beginning in front of a real audience wearing the mushy beginning.

Do it this way:

1. Walk out of the room, come back in through a real door, and give your open
   from standing position as if the room is full. Ten to fifteen minutes total.
2. Include the *physical* open: where you put your hands, where you stand, when
   you LOOK UP from the notes and say one sentence to actual humans' eyes.
3. Include the moment you will hand over to question time and ask "so, what
   questions come to mind? I'd rather take them live."

The front-door pass is worth more than all the other rehearsals combined. Do it
even if you do nothing else. (If you choose to give a chat AI your opening to
react to, chapter 7 has the exact script for that.)

## The over-rehearsal flags

Watch yourself for these. If any is true, you are past useful rehearsal:

- You are now rehearsing **word-for-word wording** of anything beyond your open
  and your landing sentence.
- You find yourself doing a **sixth** run of the same section and enjoying it
  less each time. (The enjoyment going down is a real signal.)
- Your sleep is starting to keep a playlist of your mistakes at 2 a.m.
- You now know the talk so well you feel **restlessness** with it — an itchy
  urge to change slides you were satisfied with two days ago. Itchiness is a
  sign to stop touching it, not a sign that it is broken.
- You have started "rehearsing" answers to possible audience questions that
  could go fifty different ways, each answer getting harder to remember than
  the honest "great question, I don't know yet" you were going to try anyway.

The rule that resolves all five: **remember the route, not the road.** In the
room, you will walk a slightly different road than you rehearsed — different
energy, different questions, a different mood in the audience. If you memorized
the route (three moves, signpost sentences, landing sentence), the road changing
is fine. If you memorized the road, the change is a catastrophe.

## What NOT to rehearse

- **Do not rehearse the night before or the morning of.** These rehearsals
  damage more than they polish. Do the front-door pass one day before, then
  stop.
- **Do not memorize transitions from slide to slide.** This is where slips and
  miscues happen; if you know the *idea* each slide starts with, your natural
  speaking voice builds the bridge — because that's what your natural voice has
  done in a hundred conversations.
- **Do not rehearse in a mirror.** It makes you watch yourself instead of
  practice being a person who is talking to people. The front-door pass with a
  real door and a real wall is better.
- **Do not rehearse with a friend as a stand-in audience more than once.** Their
  kindness hides problems and their non-verbal reactions teach your body nothing
  about a real room.

## If you can only do one thing

If the talk is tomorrow and you have done nothing: do the front-door pass.
Ninety seconds, said out loud, from standing, through a door you actually walk
in — and then go to bed preferring "rested and with a known route" over
"gnawed and with a memorized road."

## One-sentence summary

Rehearse three times on purpose — route, full pass, front door — with a real
recording once, and treat every additional pass as something that must pay rent
or get out.
